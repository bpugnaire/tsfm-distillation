3 · How time-series foundation models work

After this chapter you can
  • describe the five stages every TSFM goes through: scale, tokenise, mix, decode the output, roll out;
  • explain the design choices that differ between Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, Toto, t0, Timestral and our student;
  • read the student's code and map each block to its role;
  • say what pretraining data these models learn from, and why synthetic data is part of it.
In one minute
  • A foundation model is trained once on millions of series, then forecasts new ones it has never seen.
  • Inside, it always does the same five things: scale the input, cut it into patches, let the patches exchange information (a transformer), output quantiles, and convert back to real units.
  • Models differ in details: how they scale, how big the patches are, how they keep quantiles in order, how they forecast far ahead.

The idea

Intuition Classical models are fitted per series. A foundation model is trained once on millions of series from every domain (energy, traffic, sales, servers, weather), and learns generic forecasting skills: continue a cycle, extrapolate a trend, widen the band after a shock. It then forecasts a series it has never seen from its context alone: zero-shot.

Stage 1 · Scaling: make every series look alike

Intuition The network should not care whether a series is in megawatts or page views. Normalise each context by its own location and scale, forecast in that unit-free space, and convert back at the end. This is instance normalisation (RevIN in the literature).
Analogy Comparing prices in euros, yen and dollars is confusing; converting everything to one currency first makes patterns obvious. Scaling converts every series to the same "currency" (unit-free numbers around zero). At the end, the forecast is converted back.
Formally (our student) $$z_t = \operatorname{asinh}\!\left(\frac{x_t - \operatorname{median}(x)}{\operatorname{mean}\lvert x - \operatorname{median}(x)\rvert}\right) \qquad \hat{x} = \sinh(\hat{z})\cdot \text{scale} + \text{loc}$$
x
the raw context
z
what the network sees
ẑ, x̂
its forecast, in scaled then raw units
Statistics come from the context only: using the future would be leakage. asinh is ≈ z near 0 and ≈ log(2|z|) far out, so spikes are compressed instead of dominating.

The "spikes" shape from the gallery: a daily wave plus a few large outliers. Mean/std scaling lets the spikes set the scale, which squashes the wave the model has to learn. Median/MAD ignores them, and asinh tames them.

Pitfall A constant series has scale 0, so dividing by it explodes. Every implementation adds a floor (ours: 0.001 × (|loc| + 1)). Toto, t0 and Timestral all have their own variant; check yours.

Stage 2 · Tokenisation: patches or bins

Transformers read a sequence of vectors (tokens). There are two ways to turn numbers into tokens:

Analogy Reading a book letter by letter is slow and hides the meaning; reading word by word is faster and each unit carries more information. Patches are the "words" of a time series.
  • Patches (PatchTST, TimesFM, Toto, t0, Timestral, ours): cut the context into blocks of p steps and embed each block with a small MLP. 512 steps with p = 16 give 32 tokens.
  • Bins (Chronos-1): scale, then round each value to one of B bins, and treat the bin index as a word from a vocabulary. It reuses language-model machinery, at the cost of rounding error and one token per time step.

Shaded stripes: patches (one token each). Grey: the real series. Orange: the same series after binning, the only information a bin-token model receives.

Attention compares every token with every other one, so its cost grows with the square of the number of tokens. Patching 512 steps into 32 tokens makes attention 256× cheaper. That is why patches won. We also feed a "was observed" mask next to the values, so a missing value (NaN) is not mistaken for a zero.

Stage 3 · Mixing information: the transformer

Analogy A meeting where every patch can ask every other patch "do you look like me?". The patch for last Monday 9 am pays attention to the Monday 9 am of the week before, and borrows its pattern. Stacking layers is holding several rounds of that meeting.

Each layer lets every token look at the others (self-attention) and then transforms it with an MLP. After a few layers, the token for "the last patch" knows about the daily cycle seen 5 days ago and the level shift 2 days ago.

  • Decoder-only models (Toto, TimesFM) use causal attention: patch i only sees patches ≤ i, and every position is trained to predict the next patch. That gives many training signals per series.
  • Encoder models (our student) let every token see every other, and read the forecast from a dedicated "forecast token". Simpler; fine for a fixed horizon.
  • Multivariate models alternate attention over time and over variates (Toto, Timestral) or over a group of related series (Chronos-2). Our student is univariate.

Stage 4 · The output head

The last hidden state is mapped to the forecast. Three families:

  • Quantile head: K outputs per step, each trained with the pinball loss at its level (Toto 2.0, t0, Timestral, Chronos-2, ours).
  • Parametric: the parameters of a distribution (e.g. a mixture of Student-t), trained by likelihood (Moirai, Toto 1.0).
  • Categorical over bins: a softmax over the value vocabulary (Chronos-1), trained with cross-entropy.

Quantiles must not cross

Analogy Quantiles are the rungs of a ladder: the 10% rung must be below the 30% rung, which must be below the median. A head that outputs 13 numbers independently is a ladder with loose rungs; ours builds it from the middle rung and only ever adds positive gaps, so the rungs cannot swap.

Nothing forces 13 independent outputs to stay ordered. If q0.7 < q0.3, the forecast is not a valid distribution. We measured raw Chronos-2 outputs crossing in 0.4–1.6% of cells; Toto sorts its outputs after the fact. Our student builds the order in: predict the median, then add positive gaps (softplus) outwards.

raw network outputs:

Top: use the four outputs directly as q0.1, q0.3, q0.7, q0.9 (median at 0). Drag a₂ above a₃ and they cross. Bottom: the same outputs as gaps, q0.3 = −softplus(a₂), q0.1 = q0.3 − softplus(a₁), …: always ordered, whatever the network outputs.

What about levels a model does not produce?

Toto 2.0 only outputs 0.1…0.9, but we also want 0.01 and 0.99. Inside the range, interpolate. Outside, extrapolate the tails with an exponential shape fitted to the two outermost quantiles (the rule t0 uses). It is a guess, and it is flagged as such in every result file.

Stage 5 · Decoding long horizons

A model trained to output 32 patches can forecast at most 32 × 32 = 1024 steps in one pass. Beyond that, models roll out autoregressively: append the forecast median to the context as if observed, forecast the next block, repeat. Errors can compound, and the uncertainty of the appended block is lost (only its median is fed back). It is the children's game of "telephone": each player repeats what they heard, and small errors add up along the chain. Toto 2.0 and Timestral both do this "median feedback" in their decoding loops. Our 64-step horizon fits in one pass for every model.

Pretraining data

  • Real corpora: large public collections (energy, traffic, retail, finance, weather…), and for Toto, Datadog's observability metrics.
  • Synthetic data: KernelSynth draws series from Gaussian processes whose kernels are randomly composed (periodic + trend, periodic × smooth…). It provides unlimited, diverse, licence-free training data (chapter 4 has a live sampler).
  • Augmentations: TSMixup mixes several series into new ones; random scaling and cropping.
In this project: does size buy accuracy? On the fresh electricity data, Timestral-28M scores the same as Timestral-209M, and four very different models are statistically tied (see the table in chapter 2). On the toy shapes, every model has a failure mode: t0-gamma is 2.3× worse than seasonal naive on a level shift, and on impulse-decay the smallest Toto (4M) beats every larger Toto and both Chronos-2 models. Bigger is better on average, not everywhere.

Check yourself

1. Why compute the scaler's statistics on the context only?

At forecast time the future is unknown. Using it in training would let the model learn from information it cannot have, and evaluation would be optimistic (leakage).

2. A model reads 4096 steps with patches of 32. How many tokens, and how does attention cost compare with patches of 16?

128 tokens. With patches of 16: 256 tokens, so 4× the attention cost (256² / 128² = 4).

3. Two independent heads output q0.1 and q0.9. Name two ways to guarantee q0.1 ≤ q0.9.

Construct it (median + positive gaps, as in our head), or post-process it (sort the outputs, as Toto does; sorting never increases the total pinball loss).

4. Why would a model pretrained only on real data still benefit from synthetic GP data?

Real corpora are biased toward a few domains and patterns. Synthetic data fills gaps (rare periods, odd combinations of trend and season) and is unlimited, licence-free and leakage-free.
