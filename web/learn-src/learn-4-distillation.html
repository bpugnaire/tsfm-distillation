4 · Knowledge distillation for forecasters

After this chapter you can
  • explain why a teacher's forecast can be a better training target than the true future;
  • say why classic distillation (soft labels + KL divergence) does not apply directly to quantile forecasters, and what replaces it;
  • implement the three losses of this project and choose α;
  • decide which inputs to distil on, and why pure random noise does not work but structured random series do.

The idea: learn from a teacher, not only from the answers

Intuition The true future is one draw from what could have happened. If tomorrow was unusually cold, the observed load is high, but a forecast made today should not have predicted that exactly. A good teacher tells the student the whole range of plausible futures. That target is less noisy than the single observed outcome, and it is available for any input, even without ground truth.

In knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), a small student is trained to reproduce the outputs of a large teacher. For image classifiers the teacher's probability vector says "this 7 looks a bit like a 1": information the hard label "7" does not carry (dark knowledge). The student matches it with a KL divergence between temperature-softened softmaxes.

Why that recipe does not transfer

Our models have no softmax and no logits: they output 13 quantile values per step. There is nothing to soften with a temperature, and no categorical distribution to compare with a KL divergence. What the teacher does give us is its predictive distribution, seen through its quantiles. The question becomes: what loss makes the student's quantiles match the teacher's distribution?

Three losses

Lgt: learn from the truth only (the floor)

The pinball loss of chapter 2 against the observed future, summed over levels with probability-mass weights (so the 1% and 99% levels do not count as much as the median). A student trained only with it is the floor every distilled student must beat, at the same size and on the same data.

Lkd, huber: copy each quantile

The obvious approach: regress the student's qτ onto the teacher's qτ, level by level, with a robust (Huber) loss. Simple, but every level is fitted independently and every teacher mistake is copied with full weight.

Lkd, pinball: treat the teacher's quantiles as samples

Intuition A teacher's 13 quantiles, each weighted by the probability mass it represents, form a small discrete distribution: "with probability 3%, the value is near q0.01; with 10%, near the median…". Use those values as weighted pseudo-observations and train with the ordinary pinball loss. Chapter 2 showed that the pinball loss is minimised by the quantile of whatever data you give it. Give it the teacher's distribution, and the minimum is the teacher's quantile.

Orange ticks: the teacher's 13 quantiles; their height is the weight of each. The loss minimum (blue dot) lands on the teacher's τ-quantile for every τ.

Formally Lkd = Σk wk Σj vj · 2ρτk( qteacherj, qstudentk ) where vj is the probability mass of teacher level j and wk that of student level k. This is the expected pinball loss under the teacher's discretised distribution. With samples instead of quantiles it would be the "sample-pinball" loss; Toto 2.0 outputs no samples, and this version is the same loss with quantiles in their place.
Pitfall: do not distil a guess Toto 2.0 does not produce the 1% and 99% quantiles; we extrapolate them. Distilling from those extrapolated values would teach the student our guess, not the teacher's knowledge. The loss takes a mask of the teacher's native levels, and only those act as pseudo-observations.

Everything happens in the student's scaled space

The ground truth and the teacher's quantiles are transformed with the student's own scaler (computed on the context) before the loss. This is allowed because quantiles commute with increasing transforms: the 0.9-quantile of asinh(Y) is asinh of the 0.9-quantile of Y. So the minimum does not move, and a 50 GW series and a 3 GW one contribute comparable gradients.

Mixing truth and teacher: α

FormallyL = (1 − α) · Lgt + α · Lkd
  • α = 0: the teacher is ignored (the floor).
  • α = 1: pure imitation. No ground truth needed, so the student can learn on inputs that have no future at all (next section), but it can never be better than its teacher.
  • In between: the truth corrects the teacher's mistakes, and the teacher smooths the truth's noise. Where the best α lies is an experiment, not something to assume.

Inputs and targets are two different questions

Intuition Every training example is a pair: an input (the context the student reads) and a target (what it should output). "Distillation" is a statement about the target: it comes from a teacher. "Synthetic" is a statement about the input: it does not come from the real world. The two are independent, so there are four combinations.
target = the real futuretarget = a teacher's forecast
input = a real seriesordinary supervised training (our Lgt floor)classic distillation: the teacher labels real contexts
input = generated (KernelSynth, shapes, rollouts)synthetic pretraining: the generator knows the true continuation (how Chronos used KernelSynth)data-free / synthetic-input distillation: no real data at all

So yes: in distillation the target is always a teacher's output, a model-made label, whether the input was real or not. A teacher's forecast on real data is not "real data". It is a real input with a synthetic label. What changes between the rows is where in input space the student is taught, and that decides where it will be good. With α < 1 you mix the two columns: the teacher's label and the true future of the same input, which only exists in the top row or for generators that know the continuation.

Which data do we distil on? (and: can it be pure noise?)

Intuition Distillation teaches the student the teacher's function: context → forecast. A function is only learned where you show it inputs. The question is not "is the data real?" but "on these inputs, does the teacher do anything worth copying?".

With α = 1 we do not need any ground truth, only contexts. So why not generate millions of random contexts, label them with the teachers, and train on that? We measured what the teachers answer on five kinds of input:

What this shows. On white noise the teachers are right: the best forecast of independent noise is a flat line at the level with a constant band, and their 80% band is about 3.3 mean absolute deviations wide, what a perfectly calibrated forecast of Gaussian noise gives. But they give essentially the same answer to every context (diversity near 0). A student trained on that learns one thing, "predict the average with a fixed band", and never sees the teacher continue a cycle, follow a trend or react to a level shift. Random walks are only slightly better. Structured randomness is a different story: KernelSynth contexts already make the teachers move and differ almost as much as on real data.

Try it: random but structured

KernelSynth draws random series from a Gaussian process. The kernel says which shapes are likely; the randomness picks one. With no kernel ticked you get white noise. Combine a periodic kernel with a trend, or multiply it by a smooth one to get a season whose amplitude drifts.

Teacher rollouts: let the teacher write the data

Start a teacher on a real context, take one plausible continuation of its forecast, append it, forecast again, and repeat. You get very long series whose start is real and whose rest is "what the teacher believes could happen next". The catch: a forecast is a distribution, and to continue the series you must choose one path from it. We measured three choices on 24 real electricity contexts and 24 random shapes, with two teachers, over 16 blocks of 64 steps:

  • median: feed back the median (what Toto and Timestral do internally for long horizons);
  • independent: draw each step's value at random from its quantiles, independently of its neighbours;
  • correlated: draw a random path whose randomness is correlated from step to step (Gaussian copula, AR(1) with ρ = 0.9), like real noise.

Top: median over series, per generated block (1 = like the real data). Bottom: one example per strategy (Timestral-28M), real part in grey, generated part in colour. Source: scripts/rollout_study.py → results/rollouts.json.

What it shows. Median feedback makes the series progressively smoother and flatter (no noise is ever fed back). Independent sampling makes it jagged at once, and the teacher reads the jitter as noise and widens its bands, a feedback loop. Correlated sampling is realistic for the first blocks, then wanders off: every random excursion is read as signal and compounds. So rollouts are a promising generator if they stay short (a few blocks from many real starts). The rollout length is a parameter to test, not to guess. Alternating teachers between blocks is another way to diversify.

Pitfall With α = 1 and synthetic inputs, the student can at best become a copy of the teacher, and only on the kind of inputs it saw. Its quality on real data then depends on how well the synthetic distribution covers real patterns. This is a distribution shift, measurable: evaluate on the fresh real data and on the shapes.
In this project (proposed, not run) One experiment answers the question directly: the same student and the same teacher, labels on {real windows, KernelSynth, shapes, white noise}, evaluated on fresh electricity and shapes. Cost: one cache per input family (minutes on a laptop GPU) plus one short training run each.

Which teacher?

No teacher wins everywhere (see the per-shape table on Results): t0-gamma fails on level shifts, Timestral-5M on sawtooth, and the large Toto loses to its own 4M sibling on impulse-decay. A student copying one teacher inherits its blind spots. Options for multi-teacher distillation:

  • average the teachers' quantiles (simple; blurs disagreements);
  • weight each teacher per window by its error on that window's ground truth (softmax of −τ·WQL), which needs ground truth;
  • choose per level: a teacher with native tails for the 1% / 99% levels, the most accurate one for the centre.

Measure fidelity and accuracy separately

Fidelity (how close the student is to the teacher) and accuracy (how close it is to the truth) are different questions. A perfectly faithful student of a wrong teacher is still wrong. We report both.

In practice: the teacher cache

Running a 300M-parameter teacher at every training step would be far too slow. Instead, teachers run once over the training contexts and their quantiles are stored on disk (teacher cache: float16, one row per window id, resumable). Training then only reads numbers. On this project's laptop (Apple M1 Pro GPU), caching 300k windows takes about 25 minutes per teacher.

Check yourself

1. Why can a distilled student beat a student trained on the same data with ground truth only?

The teacher's distribution is a lower-variance target than the single observed future, and it encodes knowledge from the teacher's much larger pretraining corpus. The student gets more information per example.

2. With α = 1, can the student become better than the teacher on the test set?

Not by design: its target is the teacher. It may occasionally score better (regularisation, smoothing teacher noise), but there is no signal pushing it beyond the teacher. α < 1 brings the truth back in.

3. Why does distilling on white noise produce a student that cannot forecast a daily cycle?

The student only learns the teacher's function where it sees inputs. On white noise the teacher always outputs a flat, constant-width forecast, so no example ever shows a cycle being continued.

4. Why mask the teacher's extrapolated tail levels in the KD loss?

They are our extrapolation, not the teacher's output. Distilling them would teach the student our assumption about tail shape, presented as teacher knowledge.
