6 · Running an ablation study

How to find out what actually matters in a training recipe, told through our first real experiment (Stage A of the distillation study).

After this chapter you can
  • design an ablation: a reference recipe, one factor changed at a time, fixed test suites, selection on validation;
  • read a paired comparison and its interval, and say when a difference is real;
  • explain the Stage A results: when teacher labels beat the ground truth, why each data source wins at home, why noise fails;
  • recognise the sanity checks that catch silent bugs before they become conclusions.
In one minute
  • To learn what matters in a recipe, change one ingredient at a time and keep everything else fixed.
  • Compare two models on the same test windows (paired); it removes the luck of which windows are easy.
  • Choose on validation, report on test. Picking the best of many on the test set quietly inflates the result.
  • What moved the needle here: more data > fine-tuning on your history > teacher labels > a bigger student > which teacher.

What an ablation study is

Intuition A recipe has many ingredients (data, teacher, loss, size…). To know which one matters, keep everything fixed and change one ingredient. The difference in the result is that ingredient's effect. Change two at once and you can no longer tell which one did it.
Analogy Tasting a soup and wondering whether the salt or the thyme makes it good: cook two pots that differ only in the salt. Change salt and thyme together and you will never know which one it was.

Each variant is an arm. One arm is the reference. Every arm is trained the same way and scored on the same test suites, which are never used to choose anything. Choices such as "which checkpoint" or "which recipe goes to the next stage" are made on a separate validation set; otherwise the test score is optimistic (chapter 1, leakage).

Trying every combination (a full factorial) explodes quickly: 5 data sources × 5 teachers × 3 α × 3 sizes × 3 seeds = 675 runs. We work in stages instead: each stage answers one question, and its winner (chosen on validation) becomes the fixed setting of the next.

Reading the results: paired comparisons

Intuition Some test windows are easy, some are very hard. Two models compared on different windows differ partly because of luck in the windows. Compared on the same windows, the difficulty cancels: look at the ratio of their errors window by window.
Analogy Two runners timed on different days are hard to compare (wind, heat). Two runners in the same race, side by side, are easy: whoever is ahead is faster, whatever the weather.
Formally $$\text{ratio}(a, b) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\mathrm{WQL}_a(i)}{\mathrm{WQL}_b(i)}\right)$$
i
a test window (both models scored on the same ones)
exp(mean(log))
the geometric mean, so ×2 and ×½ cancel
95% interval: cluster bootstrap over series (chapter 2). Below 1, a is better. The effect is called real only if the whole interval is on one side of 1.

Pairing makes a huge difference in practice: two arms can have overlapping intervals on their own scores and still a clear, tight paired difference. Our test test_paired_ratio_ci shows a uniform 10% improvement that the unpaired intervals hide and the paired one detects.

What we learned, and why

1. On real inputs, the teacher's forecast is a better target than the real future

Teacher labels vs ground truth on the same real windows: on fresh electricity, on shapes, on the multi-domain test.

Why Every context is unique: for each one, the training data contains one observed future, a single noisy draw from what could have happened. Learning a quantile from one draw is very noisy. The teacher instead hands over its whole predictive distribution: no sampling noise, but possibly biased. It is the classic bias–variance trade-off, applied to the target.

Task: learn the 0.9-quantile of a N(0, 1) outcome. Grey: from n real observations (error shrinks like 1/√n). Orange: from the teacher's distribution N(bias, (1 + spread error)²); no noise, so its error is the same for every n. Computed live (400 repetitions per n).

In forecasting the relevant n is small (each situation is seen once), so a reasonably good teacher wins. This is our interpretation of the result, consistent with it; Stage B tests it further with three teachers and α = 0.5.

2. On synthetic inputs, teacher labels add nothing

Same comparison on synthetic contexts: on electricity, on the multi-domain test, and the ground truth even wins on shapes (). In the widget's terms: synthetic series have little noise, so the real target is already precise, while teachers make more mistakes on unusual synthetic shapes (larger bias). Move the bias slider right: the teacher stops winning.

3. Each input source wins on its own kind of data

Synthetic vs real inputs (teacher labels): on shapes (synthetic wins), on the real multi-domain test (real wins), on electricity (tie). The student learns the teacher's behaviour where it is shown inputs (chapter 4); a test that looks like the training inputs is easier. This is covariate shift. The practical answer is to mix sources, which is what Stage C does.

4. Teacher rollouts did not help (at this scale)

Rollouts vs real inputs: on the multi-domain test, on electricity. A rollout is the teacher's own idea of what comes next: training on it shows the student the teacher's "comfort zone" rather than new situations, and a few blocks of drift make contexts less realistic (chapter 4). Worth one more try at larger scale, not a priority.

5. White noise is the worst input everywhere

Noise vs synthetic inputs: on shapes, on electricity, as predicted by the teacher-on-noise measurement of chapter 4: the teacher's answer to noise is always "flat line", so there is nothing to learn.

6. The gap that remains

The best Stage A student scores on fresh electricity (1 = seasonal naive), while its teacher scores . On the multi-domain test it is much closer: vs Toto 2.0 4M at . A 0.22M-parameter student trained for 2 minutes on 30,000 windows does not generalise to a domain it never saw. Two levers remain: scale (Stage C) and fine-tuning on the target domain (Stage D).

Stage C-lite: does scale close the gap?

Stage A left a big gap on electricity. Stage C-lite changes three things at once compared with Stage A: 10× more data (300,000 windows), mixed sources (half synthetic, half real, since each source won on its own ground), and a 4× bigger student (0.95M parameters), trained for 15,000 steps. To know which change did what, it also trains three one-factor-off arms: the same recipe with ground truth instead of teacher labels, with the small student, and with only 30,000 windows.

Fair scaling More data only helps if the model actually sees it. The 30,000-window arm is trained for 1,500 steps instead of 15,000, so both make the same number of passes over their data (about 13). Otherwise "300k vs 30k" would really measure "trained 10× longer".

  • Data is the biggest lever: 300k vs 30k windows gives on shapes and on electricity. The student only learns its teacher where it is shown inputs (chapter 4). Ten times more, and more varied, contexts cover more of what it will meet at test time: less covariate shift.
  • Teacher labels still beat ground truth at scale: (shapes), (multi-domain), (electricity). This matters: a skeptic could have said "distillation only helps when data is scarce". But the target-noise argument of the widget above does not depend on the number of contexts. Each context still comes with one noisy future, however many contexts you have.
  • Size helps, but least: 0.95M vs 0.22M gives (shapes) and (electricity). At this data scale, the small model is limited more by what it sees than by its capacity. Scaling laws for language models say the same: data and size must grow together.

How sure are we? One seed. The intervals cover the randomness of the test windows, not of training: a second seed could move each arm by a few percent. Stage B: which teacher, and how much of it? (3 seeds)

Fixed: Stage A's setting (small student, 30,000 real multi-domain windows). Changed: the teacher (Timestral-209M, Toto 2.0 313m, t0-gamma, the average of the three, or a per-window weighted combination) and α (1 = teacher only, 0.5 = half teacher, half truth). Every arm is trained with 3 seeds, and contrasts use the seed-averaged scores.

Paired ratios on seed-averaged scores (below 1 = the first arm is better). Source: results/study/stage_b/table.json.

  • Teacher labels beat the truth, confirmed with 3 seeds: on electricity, on shapes. With one seed this could have been luck; with three, it is the most robust result of the study.
  • Which teacher matters little: Toto vs Timestral , t0 vs Timestral (electricity). Surprise: t0-gamma is the best zero-shot model on the multi-domain test, yet the worst teacher for electricity. A teacher's value is how good its labels are on the inputs the student trains on, and how well those transfer to the target, not its leaderboard rank.
  • Averaging the three teachers helps a little: (electricity), (multi-domain). Averaging quantiles level by level cancels part of each teacher's individual errors, the same reason ensembles help everywhere in ML.
  • Weighting teachers per window by their error does not help: vs the plain average. The weights are computed from each window's one observed future, the same noisy evidence the teacher labels were meant to smooth. The weights chase the noise. (t0 was the per-window "best" teacher on 55% of windows, and that still did not translate into a better student.)
  • α = 1 is best: α = 0.5 vs 1 gives (Timestral) and (ensemble) on electricity. Mixing the noisy truth back in only adds noise to an already better target. Even for Toto, whose 1%/99% levels are not native, α = 0.5 brings almost nothing.
Seeds, in numbers Retraining the same arm with another seed moves its score by about ±0.005 on the multi-domain test but by ±0.02–0.10 on shapes and electricity. So on those suites, a single-seed difference below ~5% says nothing. Rule of thumb: estimate the seed spread before trusting small effects.

Stage D: a light fine-tuning on your data

The headline question. Take the best student so far (Stage C-lite, chosen on validation), and fine-tune it on a target domain's own recent history (1 week, 1 month or 1 year), with plain supervised training on the true future. Compare with the same architecture trained from scratch on that history, and with the big models used as-is. Two domains it never saw in distillation: French electricity, and Melbourne pedestrian counts.

"1 week of your data" has two meanings Warm: you have a long past, but only label the last week for fine-tuning (contexts can use the full past). Cold start: you only have a week. Then contexts are short at training and at test time, and the big models must be scored on the same short contexts to be fair. Our pilot mixed the two (short training contexts, long test contexts) and got misleading failures. The final stage separates them.

Stage E: teacher labels on your history

Stage D fine-tuned on your history with the real future as target. But Stages A–C showed that a teacher's forecast is a better target than the real future. So Stage E keeps everything identical (same starting student, history lengths, schedule and seeds) and changes only the fine-tuning target: Timestral-209M's forecast of each window of your history (kd, α = 1), or a 50/50 mix with the real future (α = 0.5). In practice the big model runs once, offline, over your history (about 10 minutes on a laptop CPU for 73,000 windows); then the small model is all you deploy.

1. The prediction held: the "bad month" stops being bad

Stage D's anomaly: fine-tuning on the last month of electricity with the real future made the student worse than no fine-tuning (). Our diagnosis was that one month is a noisy, idiosyncratic sample. With teacher labels on the same month, the student is vs the real-future version, and fine-tuning now helps ( vs no fine-tuning). This is the target-noise argument of the widget above, applied to your own data: each window of your month has one noisy realised future, and the teacher replaces it with its distribution, which keeps what is typical and drops what is accidental.

Why this is a good scientific moment We had a hypothesis (target noise), it made a prediction about an experiment not yet run ("teacher labels should fix the 1-month case"), and the experiment could have refuted it. It did not. That is far stronger evidence than explaining a result after the fact.

2. Teacher labels make your data go 12× further

Teacher labels on 1 month of history vs the real future on 1 year: (electricity), (pedestrians): no detectable difference. The gain is largest when supervision is short or noisy (pedestrians, 1 week: ) and fades with a year of data (), when the real futures average out on their own.

3. The headline, chosen honestly

Three targets × three history lengths make nine candidates. Which one do we report? The one the fine-tuning validation set picks (the last 20% of your history, never the test). For electricity with a year of data, validation picks pure teacher labels: , i.e. vs Toto 2.0 4m (better, with 4.3× fewer parameters), vs Timestral-5M, and vs its 209M-parameter teacher. Pedestrians (validation picks the mix): vs Toto 2.0 4m, vs Timestral-5M.

Analogy Taking an exam nine times and reporting only your best grade tells people more about luck than about you. Choosing on validation is deciding which attempt counts before seeing the grades.
The 4% we did not claim Reading all nine candidates on the test set, the best one (the 50/50 mix with a year of electricity) scores vs Timestral-5M: a tie. It is tempting to headline "matches Timestral-5M". But it was chosen because it looked best on test, so its score is optimistically biased (the maximum of nine noisy numbers). The validation-selected recipe is about 4% worse. That gap is the size of the lie you would tell by selecting on test.

Sanity checks that saved the study

Experiments fail silently far more often than they crash. What caught our bugs:

  • Look at anything suspicious. Two different arms had exactly the same best validation score. That turned out to be a coincidence, but checking the logs revealed that another arm's distillation loss was inf: teacher labels were stored in float16, which overflows above 65,504, and 3–5% of real-data labels had become infinite. Training did not crash, because the pinball loss's gradient stays finite. Fixed, with a regression test, and the affected arms were re-run.
  • Integrity checks must test what can actually go wrong. The cache check looked for NaN, not for infinity. It now rejects any non-finite value.
  • Run a tiny version first. A 1-minute smoke run of the whole pipeline caught three bugs before the real run: arms of unequal size, a stale cache silently reused, and a float64 array the Mac GPU cannot hold.
  • Log every component of the loss, not just the total. The inf was visible only in the "kd" column.
  • Never silently fix a model's output. In Stage B, Toto 2.0 313m returned non-finite values on the Mac GPU for one perfectly flat series (fine on CPU). The adapter's check refused the output and stopped the run, instead of quietly producing a corrupted teacher. Lesson: changing where a teacher runs can change the teacher.

Check yourself

1. Why choose the recipe for the next stage on the validation set and not on the test suites?

Choosing on the test set turns it into training data for decisions: the reported test score of the chosen recipe becomes optimistically biased. The test must be read only to report.

2. Two arms score 0.76 [0.69, 0.82] and 0.73 [0.69, 0.78]; their paired ratio is 0.97 [0.93, 1.01]. Conclusion?

No detectable difference: the paired interval includes 1. (These are the Stage A numbers for teacher vs ground truth on the multi-domain test.)

3. Using the bias–variance widget: when would ground truth beat a teacher even with one observation per context?

When the outcome is nearly noise-free (the real future is already the right target) or when the teacher is badly biased on that kind of data, as on unusual synthetic shapes.

4. Why does the 30k-window arm of Stage C-lite train for 1,500 steps and not 15,000?

To compare data amounts at the same number of passes over the data. With 15,000 steps on 30k windows it would see each window ten times more often: the comparison would mix "more data" with "less overfitting control".

5. Teacher labels beat ground truth by 15% at 30k windows. Should we expect the gain to vanish at 300k?

Not if the gain comes from target noise: each context still has a single observed future at any scale. Stage C-lite measured 4–15%, consistent with that.

6. In Stage E, why is the reported electricity recipe "teacher labels, 1 year" and not the 50/50 mix that scored best on test?

Because the choice has to be made without looking at the test set. The fine-tuning validation set picks teacher labels; picking the test winner among nine candidates would bias the reported number upward (here by about 4%).

7. Teacher labels on 1 month ≈ real future on 1 year. In which situation would you expect this to break?

If the teacher is poor on your domain (biased), its labels inject that bias, and a long, clean history of real futures would win. The widget on target noise shows the trade-off: teacher bias vs the noise of single observations.

8. Why is a 1-seed result called a "pilot"?

Training randomness (initialisation, batch order) moves scores too. An effect smaller than the seed-to-seed spread can be noise; headline results use 3 seeds (Stage B onwards).
