2 · Probabilistic forecasts and how to score them

After this chapter you can
  • explain what a quantile forecast is and why the pinball loss trains one;
  • tell calibration from sharpness, and see why a good score needs both;
  • compute CRPS / WQL and MASE, and know the hidden bias of the 9-level "CRPS" used by benchmarks;
  • put error bars on a score, and know when they are too optimistic.
In one minute
  • Good forecasts say how sure they are: "between 58 and 66 GW, most likely 62".
  • We describe that uncertainty with quantiles, and train them with the pinball loss: a penalty that is asymmetric on purpose.
  • A good forecast is calibrated (its 80% bands contain the truth 80% of the time) and sharp (the bands are narrow).
  • One number (CRPS / WQL) rewards both at once. And every score needs an error bar.

Why forecast a distribution?

Intuition A grid operator does not need "tomorrow 19:00 = 62 GW". It needs "how much reserve should I book so that I am short at most once a month?". That question is about the upper tail of the distribution, not about its centre.

A point forecast gives one number per step. A probabilistic forecast gives a whole distribution. All models in this project describe it with 13 quantiles per step, at levels τ = 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, …, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99. The 0.9-quantile q0.9 is the value the truth should exceed only 10% of the time. The bands you see in every chart (40%, 80%, 98%) are pairs of quantiles.

Analogy Your weather app does not say "it will rain 3 mm"; it says "70% chance of rain". That percentage is the uncertainty. Quantiles are the same idea for numbers: "90% chance it stays below 66 GW" is the 0.9-quantile.

Quantiles and the pinball loss

Analogy: the shopkeeper A baker decides how many croissants to bake. Running out costs her 9 lost sales per missing croissant; leftovers cost 1 each. The best plan is not the average demand: she should bake enough to run out only 10% of the days, the 0.9-quantile of demand. The pinball loss is exactly her cost: under-forecasting is 9× more expensive than over-forecasting at τ = 0.9.
Intuition To learn the 0.9-quantile, make under-forecasting 9 times more expensive than over-forecasting. The cheapest place to stand is then the point that is exceeded exactly 10% of the time.
Formally $$\rho_\tau(y, q) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y - q) & \text{if } y \ge q \quad\text{(we forecast too low)}\\ (1-\tau)\,(q - y) & \text{otherwise} \quad\text{(we forecast too high)}\end{cases}$$
y
what happened
q
our forecast of the τ-quantile
τ
the level, e.g. 0.9
For a random outcome Y, the q that minimises E[ρτ(Y, q)] is exactly the τ-quantile of Y. (Derivative: −τ·P(Y > q) + (1 − τ)·P(Y < q) = 0 ⇔ P(Y < q) = τ.) At τ = 0.5 it is half the absolute error, minimised by the median.

300 observations from a skewed distribution (orange ticks). Blue: the average pinball loss if you always forecast q. Its minimum lands on the τ-quantile of the data (dashed).

A network with 13 outputs trained with the sum of 13 pinball losses learns 13 quantiles at once. That is the "quantile head" of Toto 2.0, t0, Chronos-2, Timestral and our student.

Calibration and sharpness

Analogy: the honest friend A friend who says "I'm 90% sure" about many things should be right about 9 times in 10. If they are right only 6 times in 10, they are over-confident (not calibrated). A friend who answers every question with "somewhere between 0 and a million" is always right and never useful (not sharp).

Two properties make a probabilistic forecast good:

  • Calibration: the probabilities are honest. An 80% interval contains the truth about 80% of the time (its coverage).
  • Sharpness: the intervals are narrow. "Tomorrow between 0 and 100 GW" is calibrated and useless.

The principle (Gneiting et al.): maximise sharpness subject to calibration. A good score must reward both, and a forecaster must not be able to game it.

The truth is N(0, 1). Your forecast is N(bias, spread²). Left: both densities. Right: the expected CRPS for every spread at the current bias. Find the minimum.

Try it: with bias 0, shrink the spread: the interval gets sharper but its coverage drops below 80% and the CRPS goes up. Widen it past 1: perfect coverage, worse CRPS. The minimum is at spread 1, the true distribution. Now add a bias: the best spread becomes larger than 1, since honest extra uncertainty is the right answer when you know you might be off.

In this project On the fresh electricity data, every foundation model under-covers: their 80% intervals contain only 66–71% of the outcomes (see the table further down). They are over-confident on new data.

CRPS and WQL

Intuition Score the whole distribution by averaging the pinball loss over every quantile level. A forecast that is sharp and calibrated wins at every level.
Formally The continuous ranked probability score is a proper scoring rule: $$\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(F(z) - \mathbb{1}\{y \le z\}\big)^2\,dz \;=\; 2\int_0^1 \rho_\tau\big(y, F^{-1}(\tau)\big)\,d\tau$$
F
the forecast distribution (its CDF)
F⁻¹(τ)
its τ-quantile
right-hand side
"average the pinball loss over every level"
With only K quantiles, approximate the integral by an average over levels, and divide by Σ|y| so that series of different sizes are comparable. That is the weighted quantile loss: $$\mathrm{WQL} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{2\sum_t \rho_{\tau_k}\big(y_t, q_{\tau_k,t}\big)}{\sum_t \lvert y_t\rvert}$$
K
number of quantile levels (9 for GIFT-Eval)
t
the forecast steps
denominator
makes the score unit-free

The hidden bias of "CRPS" in benchmarks

GIFT-Eval and most leaderboards compute this average over the 9 levels 0.1…0.9 and call it CRPS. Is that a good approximation? We tested it on a perfectly calibrated Gaussian forecast, where the exact answer is known:

Why At the right quantile, the expected pinball loss is σ·φ(zτ): large at the median, tiny in the tails. Using only 0.1…0.9 drops the small tail terms, so the average is too high. Our 13-level grid has extra tail levels with equal weight, so it is too low. Weighting each level by the probability mass it represents (midpoint rule) removes both biases.

Practical consequence: comparing models with the 9-level score is fine (everyone has the same bias), but our training loss weights levels by probability mass, so the student does not over-invest in the 1% and 99% tails.

MASE and relative scores

MASE scores the median forecast. It divides the forecast's mean absolute error by the in-sample error of a seasonal naive on the context, so it is scale-free and readable: 0.8 means "80% of the naive's typical error".

To aggregate over many series we use relative scores: per series, model WQL ÷ seasonal-naive WQL, then a geometric mean. The geometric mean treats "twice as good" and "twice as bad" symmetrically (log 0.5 = −log 2), and one exploding series cannot dominate.

How sure are we about the score?

Analogy Measure a runner on one race and you learn about the runner and about that day's wind. The bootstrap "replays" the season many times by redrawing races from the ones you saw, to see how much the ranking could move by luck alone.

A score computed on 300 windows is itself a random quantity. The bootstrap estimates its uncertainty: resample the evaluation units with replacement, recompute the score, repeat 2000 times, and read the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles.

Pitfall Which units you resample matters. Our fresh test set has 312 windows but only 13 series, and consecutive windows of one series overlap (daily origins, 64-hour horizon). Resampling windows as if independent gave intervals that were too narrow. The cluster bootstrap resamples whole series instead: the effective sample size is closer to 13 than to 312.

Read the table like a scientist: Timestral-209M, Timestral-28M, Toto-313m and Chronos-2 are tied, because their intervals overlap almost entirely. Ranking them 1, 2, 3, 4 would be over-reading noise.

Check yourself

1. A model's 80% intervals contain the truth 95% of the time. Good or bad?

Bad: it is under-confident (intervals too wide). It is calibrated "the safe way" but not sharp, and its CRPS/WQL is worse than it could be.

2. Why can't you "game" the pinball loss by always forecasting the median for every level?

Because it is a proper scoring rule: for each τ the expected loss is minimised only at the true τ-quantile. Putting the 0.9 level at the median costs 0.9·(y − q) on the 50% of outcomes above it.

3. Model A has relative WQL 0.62 [0.58, 0.66], model B 0.64 [0.60, 0.69] on the same windows. Is A better?

Not demonstrated. The intervals overlap a lot. A paired comparison (bootstrap of the per-series difference A − B) is the right test, and it may still be inconclusive with 13 series.

4. Why divide by Σ|y| in the WQL, and when does that break?

So that a 50 GW national series and a 3 GW region count equally. It breaks when Σ|y| is near zero (series around 0, intermittent demand): the score explodes. Relative scores against a baseline reduce the problem.
