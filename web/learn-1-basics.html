1 · Forecasting fundamentals

After this chapter you can
  • name the parts of a forecasting problem (context, origin, horizon) and read any forecast plot;
  • recognise trend, seasonality, level shifts and noise, and find a season with the autocorrelation function;
  • explain why "repeat last week" is a serious baseline, and why a model must beat it;
  • set up an evaluation that does not cheat (backtesting, time splits, leakage, zero-shot vs in-domain).
In one minute
  • A forecast is a guess about the future made from a window of the past.
  • Real series mix a few ingredients: a slow trend, repeating seasons, sudden shifts, and noise.
  • Always compare with a dumb baseline ("same as last week"). It is surprisingly hard to beat.
  • Test like it is the past: only use data before the forecast date, and never test on data the model may have memorised.

The anatomy of a forecast

Intuition You stand at a point in time, look back at what happened, and say something about what comes next. Everything in forecasting is about where you stand, how far you look back and how far you look ahead.

A time series is a sequence x1, x2, … sampled at a fixed step (here: hourly electricity consumption). The forecast origin T is the last step you observed. The context is the window of past values the model reads (ours: up to 512 hours ≈ 3 weeks). The horizon H is how many steps you forecast (ours: 64 hours ≈ 2.7 days).

Analogy A weather forecaster at a window: the origin is the moment she looks up, the context is what she remembers of the last few days, the horizon is how far ahead her bulletin goes. A better forecaster is not one who remembers more, but one who knows which memories matter.
Formally A forecaster is a function f : (xT−L+1, …, xT) ↦ prediction of (xT+1, …, xT+H), with context length L and horizon H. The prediction can be one number per step (point forecast) or a distribution per step (probabilistic forecast, chapter 2).

Grey: the past. Shaded: the last 256 steps, the context a model would read. Dashed: what actually happens next. Blue: seasonal naive (repeat the last season). Orange: repeat the last value.

Try it: set the noise to 0 and move the origin: seasonal naive is perfect as long as there is no trend. Add a trend: it lags behind. Tick "level shift" and put the origin a few steps after t = 300: seasonal naive copies a "last season" that is partly before the jump, so part of its forecast sits at the old level. Move the origin 30 steps further: the last season is entirely after the jump and it recovers.

Patterns: trend, seasonality, shifts, noise

Most real series combine a few ingredients:

  • Trend: slow change of the level (growth, decline, saturation).
  • Seasonality: a pattern repeating with a fixed period m. Electricity has two: daily (m = 24) and weekly (m = 168).
  • Level shifts and trend breaks: sudden structural changes (a factory opens, a sensor is recalibrated).
  • Noise: what no model can predict. Probabilistic forecasts exist to describe it honestly.

A series whose statistical properties stay constant over time is stationary. Trends, random walks and level shifts break stationarity, which is why every model normalises the context (chapter 3).

Finding the season: the autocorrelation function

Intuition Shift the series by k steps and lay it over itself. If the copies line up, the series repeats every k steps.
Analogy Two transparencies of the same song's waveform: slide one over the other. When the beats line up again you have found the bar length. The ACF does that sliding for every possible shift and scores how well they line up.
Formally $$\mathrm{ACF}(k) = \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar{x})(x_{t+k} - \bar{x})}{\sum_t (x_t - \bar{x})^2}$$
k
the lag (how far we shift)
x̄
the average
result
1 = perfect overlap, 0 = no relation
A season of period m shows up as a peak at k = m (and smaller ones at 2m, 3m…). We remove a linear trend first, otherwise the trend makes every lag look correlated.

Pitfall On real hourly electricity, the ACF sometimes prefers the weekly period (168). A model that can only handle seasons up to 24 (statsforecast's ETS) then silently fell back to "no season" in our runs. We now record every such fallback in the results file. An automatic choice made for you deserves to be logged.

Baselines: the models to beat

Before trusting a 200-million-parameter model, compare it with something trivial:

Analogy "Tomorrow at 9 am will look like today at 9 am" is the forecasting equivalent of guessing that tomorrow's weather will be like today's. It is right surprisingly often. A sophisticated model that cannot beat it is not useful, however impressive it sounds.
  • Naive: tomorrow = today's last value.
  • Seasonal naive: copy the most recent value with the same phase. For hourly data with m = 24, the forecast for tomorrow 9:00 is today 9:00.
  • ETS (exponential smoothing): a classical statistical model with level, trend and season components, fitted per series.
Formally (seasonal naive) $$\hat{x}_{T+s} = x_{\,T + s - m\lceil s/m \rceil}, \qquad s = 1, \dots, H$$
T
now
s
steps ahead
m
the season length (24 for daily cycles in hourly data)
⌈s/m⌉ is how many whole seasons you must step back to land on an observed value with the same phase.
Pitfall (a real bug in this project) Our first version stepped back one season too many for every step beyond the first season (step 25 copied 48 h ago instead of 24 h ago). It was still a plausible forecast, so nothing crashed, but it made the baseline weaker, and every model's score relative to it looked better than it was. We found it by showing the code in this course. The fix came with a test computed by hand: x = [0, 1, 2, 10, 11, 12, 20, 21, 22], m = 3 must forecast 20, 21, 22, 20, 21, 22, …

Evaluating without cheating

The only honest test is to pretend to be in the past. A backtest picks many origins T, forecasts from each using only data up to T, and compares with what happened after T. Splits are by time, never at random: training data must end before validation starts, and validation before test.

The rule in this project applies to the forecast targets: a window belongs to a split only if its whole horizon lies inside that split's dates. Contexts may reach back into earlier periods, which is simply the past.

Leakage, the foundation-model version

Analogy Testing a forecaster on data it was trained on is like grading a student on the exact exam questions they practised with. Our "fresh" test set is an exam written after the student finished studying.

Leakage is any path by which test information influences the model. Classic forms: normalising with statistics from the future, choosing a checkpoint on the test set. Foundation models add one: they are pretrained on huge public corpora, so the benchmark you test on may be in their training data (contamination). Our answer: the headline test uses electricity data published after every model's release date, enforced by an automated test.

Zero-shot vs in-domain

A foundation model forecasting our electricity data has never been trained on it: zero-shot. Our preview student was trained on earlier years of the same 13 regions: in-domain. In-domain is usually easier, so "the small student beats the big model" means little until both are compared under the same conditions.

Check yourself

1. Why do we split time series by date and not at random?

Random splits put future observations in the training set. The model then learns from information it would never have at forecast time, and the score is optimistic.

2. Hourly data with a strong daily cycle and no trend: which simple baseline should be hard to beat, and why?

Seasonal naive with m = 24: it reproduces the daily shape exactly and only misses noise and slow changes.

3. A paper reports that a foundation model beats all baselines on a public benchmark released in 2019. What should you ask before believing it?

Whether that benchmark (or its source data) was part of the model's pretraining corpus, and how the baselines were configured (season, horizon). A fresh test set published after the model avoids the first problem.

4. Why does the ACF need the trend removed first?

A trend makes values far apart in time differ systematically, and values close in time similar, at every lag. The ACF then decays slowly and hides the seasonal peaks.
